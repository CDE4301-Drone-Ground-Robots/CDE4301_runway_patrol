1.0 Subsystem Scope & Operational Constraints

Dry Tarmac ($\mu = 0.80$) • $R_{\min} \ge 1.50\text{ m}$

1.1 Operational Environment: Airfield Runway Sweep on Dry Pavement ($\mu = 0.80$)

The autonomous Foreign Object Debris (FOD) remediation rover is engineered specifically for active airfield runway, taxiway, and apron infrastructure under statutory civil aviation regulations (Federal Aviation Administration, 2019 [2]; ASTM International, 2020 [1]; Shan et al., 2025 [12]). Runways feature standard geometric profiles of $45\text{ to }60\text{ m}$ in width and $3000\text{ to }4000\text{ m}$ in length, paved in grooved dense-graded asphalt or brushed Portland cement concrete exhibiting high surface friction ($\mu \approx 0.80$ under dry ambient conditions).

Operating on active runways imposes strict engineering boundaries: sweeps must execute rapidly within tight air traffic inter-arrival windows (requiring transit sprints $\ge 10.0\text{ km/h}$ or $2.78\text{ m/s}$), while retaining absolute millimeter-to-centimeter precision to reliably capture metallic fasteners, wire fragments, and tire chunks (Munyer et al., 2022 [10]).

●The Open Runway Scan-Matching Degeneracy Problem

Standard 2D LiDAR scan matching minimizes the point-to-plane residual error between successive laser scans:

$$E(\Delta \mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \left\| \mathbf{n}_k^T \Big( \mathbf{R}(\Delta \theta) \mathbf{p}_k + \Delta \mathbf{t} - \mathbf{q}_k \Big) \right\|^2$$

Along a $4000\text{ m}$ runway strip, vertical landmarks (buildings, walls) are absent beyond the runway edge markers. Consequently, the longitudinal spatial intensity gradient $\nabla I_x \approx 0$, causing the LiDAR scan-matching Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{J}$ to suffer singular value collapse:

$$\text{det}(\mathbf{H}) = \lambda_x \lambda_y \lambda_\theta \approx 0 \implies \mathbf{\Sigma}_x = (\mathbf{H}^{-1})_{xx} \to \infty$$

Under pure laser odometry or ICP, longitudinal position drifts unbounded along $+X$. Therefore, the navigation subsystem strictly mandates single-antenna RTK-GNSS pose graph priors ($\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{prior}}$) and georeferenced orthophoto costmap constraints as formulated in modern graph SLAM paradigms (Macenski & Jambrecic, 2021 [21]).

1.2 Platform Dynamics: 4WD Skid-Steer Kinematic Envelope ($R_{\min} \ge 1.50\text{ m}$)

The mobile chassis utilizes a 4-Wheel Drive (4WD) skid-steer architecture (modeled after the AgileX Scout 2.0 heavy-duty mobile base). Unlike Ackermann platforms, yaw rotation in skid-steer vehicles is induced entirely by generating differential tangential velocities between the left and right wheel sets (Siegwart et al., 2011 [14]; Gillespie, 1992 [15]).

Figure 1.1: AgileX Scout 2.0 4WD Skid-Steer Base ($65.0\text{ kg}$)
Figure 1.2: Instantaneous Center of Rotation (ICR) Kinematics

Given effective track width $B = 0.582\text{ m}$, wheelbase $L = 0.498\text{ m}$, and wheel radius $r = 0.1315\text{ m}$, the relationship between forward speed $v$, yaw rate $\omega$, and individual side wheel velocities $[v_L, v_R]$ is:

$$v = \frac{v_R + v_L}{2}, \quad \omega = \frac{v_R - v_L}{B_{\text{eff}}}, \quad R = \frac{v}{\omega} = \frac{B_{\text{eff}}}{2} \left( \frac{v_R + v_L}{v_R - v_L} \right)$$

Where $B_{\text{eff}} \approx 1.35 \cdot B$ accounts for tire slip during skid maneuvers.

Phase 1 Interim Scope Boundary vs. Post-Interim Hardware Implementation:

Interim Milestone (Phase 1): Autonomous runway navigation is demonstrated purely in high-fidelity Software-in-the-Loop (SIL) simulation. In simulation, an absolute non-holonomic turning constraint of $R_{\min} \ge 1.50\text{ m}$ is enforced to ensure smooth, high-speed ($\ge 10\text{ km/h}$) trajectory tracking without the simulated tire scrub and odometry divergence that occurs under ODE friction models.

Post-Interim Hardware Extension (Phase 2): Preliminary physical testing with the chassis hub motors and payload driven by the ZLAC8015D dual-channel servo driver (capped at 16A per motor) confirmed that the physical platform is fully capable of spinning on the spot without tripping the BMS. This enables a dual-mode control strategy (high-speed Dubins sweeps + low-speed in-place rotation for terminal debris docking and recovery), which will be deployed on the physical vehicle after the interim report.

Iterative Design & Benchmarking

Developing an autonomous navigation architecture for a heavy $65.0\text{ kg}$ skid-steer rover operating at $\ge 10.0\text{ km/h}$ on high-friction runway surfaces required an iterative exploration across the mobile robotics planning taxonomy (Dolgov et al., 2010 [19]; Dubins, 1957 [20]; Macenski et al., 2020 [8]):

Iteration 1: 2D Grid + DWB Tested & Rejected

Employed default ROS 2 NavFn ($A^*$/Dijkstra) with Dynamic Window Approach (DWB). NavFn produced 2D piecewise linear paths with sharp $45^\circ/90^\circ$ turns. DWB commanded abrupt in-place pivots from a dead stop against static breakaway friction ($\mu_s \approx 0.80$, requiring $19.73\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$), which pushed unconstrained current demand toward $74.6\text{ A}$ total and caused extreme simulated wheel-slip and odometric drift under Gazebo ODE physics.

Iteration 2: SmacPlanner2D + Smoother & RPP Phase 1 Baseline

Deployed nav2_smac_planner::SmacPlanner2D with internal cost-aware non-linear curve smoothing ($w_{\text{smooth}}=0.3, w_{\text{curve}}=0.15, w_{\text{cost}}=0.05$) paired with Regulated Pure Pursuit (RPP) at $20\text{ Hz}$ featuring dynamic curvature and cost-regulated velocity scaling (Macenski et al., 2024 [22]). Eliminated high-friction in-place pivot commands during Phase 1 benchtop and SIL testing while achieving sub-decimeter path adherence at $10\text{ km/h}$.

Iteration 3: SmacPlannerHybrid Dubins Lattice & Safety Polygons Phase 2 Implementation

Transitions to continuous $SE(2)$ SmacPlannerHybrid with Dubins motion primitives enforcing $R_{\min} \ge 1.50\text{ m}$ directly in the graph search tree. Integrates `nav2_collision_monitor` for direct $50\text{ Hz}$ pointcloud safety polygons (`PolygonStop` at $0.50\text{ m}$, `PolygonSlow` at $1.20\text{ m}$), bypassing costmap latency, with non-holonomic reverse recovery routines.

Iteration 4: Tensorized GPU MPPI Sem 2 Evaluation

Benchmarked Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) running 2,048 stochastic trajectory rollouts at $50\text{ Hz}$ on Nvidia Jetson Orin GPU (Williams et al., 2017 [23]). Provides superior dynamic obstacle swerving around ground service vehicles, but consumes $65\%$ GPU budget; reserved for complex apron operations.

2.2 Kinematic Principles & The 3 Distinct Conditions Triggering Zero-Radius Turns

The AgileX Scout 2.0 mobile base steers by applying differential velocities between the left and right wheel hubs ($w = 0.582\text{ m}$). In a zero-radius in-place rotation ($R = 0$), forward linear velocity is zero ($v = 0\text{ m/s}$) and the Instantaneous Center of Rotation (ICR) collapses onto the chassis geometric center:

$$v_R = -v_L = \omega \cdot \frac{w}{2} \implies v_R = -v_L = 1.2\text{ rad/s} \times \frac{0.582\text{ m}}{2} \approx \pm 0.35\text{ m/s}$$

While zero-radius turning causes tire scrub if abused during high-speed transit, it is strategically activated in three deterministic operational conditions:

Condition 1: Initial Path Alignment RPP Controller

Trigger: When a new goal is dispatched and the heading error between vehicle heading and planned path start exceeds $|\Delta\theta| > 45^\circ$ ($0.785\text{ rad}$).

use_rotate_to_heading: True
rotate_to_heading_min_angle: 0.785
rotate_to_heading_angular_vel: 1.2 rad/s

Benefit: Prevents wide clumsy swings or drifting off runway boundaries when commanded to travel in reverse.

Condition 2: Arrival Yaw Alignment Goal Checker

Trigger: When the rover arrives within $xy \le 0.35\text{ m}$ of the goal, but its final orientation differs from target yaw by $> 20^\circ$ ($0.35\text{ rad}$).

xy_goal_tolerance: 0.35 m
yaw_goal_tolerance: 0.35 rad (20°)
stateful: True

Benefit: Accurately aims the inspection camera or aligns vacuum intake head directly over identified debris.

Condition 3: Recovery Sensor Sweep Behavior Server

Trigger: When vehicle progress stalls for $> 10.0\text{ s}$ due to dynamic obstacles, triggering the recovery tree.

plugin: "nav2_behaviors::Spin"
max_rotational_vel: 1.0 rad/s
rotational_acc_lim: 3.2 rad/s^2

Benefit: Sweeps 2D LiDAR around $360^\circ$ to clear stale costmap rays and unwedge the robot autonomously.

3.0 Physics & Drivetrain Constraints: Tarmac Scrub Mechanics

BMS Protection • $I_{\text{bus}} < 70\text{ A}$

3.1 Breakaway Scrubbing Torque Derivation on High-Friction Asphalt

When a $65.0\text{ kg}$ 4WD skid-steer vehicle executes a zero-radius in-place rotation on dry airport asphalt ($\mu \approx 0.80$), the tires must continuously shear laterally across the pavement surface (Siegwart et al., 2011 [14]; Gillespie, 1992 [15]). The normal force per wheel is $F_{N,i} = \frac{m \cdot g}{4}$. Assuming Coulomb friction and a gearbox reduction efficiency $\eta_{\text{gear}} = 0.85$, the breakaway lateral scrub torque reflected at each motor shaft is derived as:

$$\tau_{\text{skid, per motor}} = \frac{m \cdot g \cdot \mu \cdot r}{4 \cdot \eta_{\text{gear}}} = \frac{65.0 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.80 \cdot 0.1315}{4 \cdot 0.85} \approx \mathbf{19.73\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$$

Where vehicle mass $m = 65.0\text{ kg}$, gravitational acceleration $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$, tire friction coefficient $\mu = 0.80$, effective tire radius $r = 0.1315\text{ m}$, and planetary gearbox transmission efficiency $\eta_{\text{gear}} = 0.85$.

3.2 Electrical Bus Loading: ZLAC8015D 16A Current Clamping & In-Place Pivot Feasibility

Given the chassis brushless hub motor torque constant $K_t \approx 1.057\text{ N}\cdot\text{m/A}$ (RobotPlatform.com, 2024 [17]), an unconstrained breakaway against static asphalt friction ($\tau_{\text{skid}} \approx 19.73\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$) would theoretically demand:

$$I_{\text{demand}} = \frac{\tau_{\text{skid}}}{K_t} = \frac{19.73\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{1.057\text{ N}\cdot\text{m/A}} \approx 18.66\text{ A/motor} \implies I_{\text{bus, unconstrained}} = 4 \times 18.66\text{ A} = \mathbf{74.64\text{ A}}$$

An unrestricted $74.64\text{ A}$ draw across the $48.0\text{ V}$ bus ($3.58\text{ kW}$) would breach the onboard Smart BMS continuous discharge limit ($70.0\text{ A}$), inducing acute voltage sag ($< 42.0\text{ V}$) and triggering BMS brownout shutdowns.

●Hardware Protection: ZLAC8015D 16.0 A Firmware Current Ceiling

To eliminate this failure mode at the electrical hardware layer, the ZLAC8015D V4.2 dual-channel servo driver firmware parameter register is configured with a strict current limit of $16.0\text{ A}$ per motor. This parameter clamp establishes a deterministic ceiling on both motor torque and DC bus loading:

$$\tau_{\max} = K_t \cdot I_{\max} = 1.057\text{ N}\cdot\text{m/A} \times 16.0\text{ A} = \mathbf{16.91\text{ N}\cdot\text{m/motor}}$$
$$I_{\text{bus, capped}} = 2 \times 16.0\text{ A} = \mathbf{32.0\text{ A}}\text{ (Dual Hubs)} \quad \Big[\text{or } 4 \times 16.0\text{ A} = \mathbf{64.0\text{ A}}\text{ for 4WD}\Big] < I_{\text{BMS, trip}} = 70.0\text{ A}$$

●In-Place Turning Feasibility: Static Breakaway vs. Kinetic Shearing Mechanics

Physical benchtop and chassis testing with payload verified that the physical platform is capable of turning on the spot. While static breakaway friction from a dead stop requires $19.73\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, as soon as tire shearing initiates, the contact interface transitions from static ($\mu_s \approx 0.80$) to dynamic kinetic friction ($\mu_k \approx 0.55\text{--}0.65$):

$$\tau_{\text{kinetic}} = \frac{m \cdot g \cdot \mu_k \cdot r}{4 \cdot \eta_{\text{gear}}} = \frac{65.0 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.60 \cdot 0.1315}{4 \cdot 0.85} \approx \mathbf{14.80\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$$

Because the capped motor torque ($\mathbf{16.91\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$) under the ZLAC8015D driver's $16.0\text{ A}$ current ceiling exceeds the kinetic friction torque ($\approx \mathbf{14.80\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}$), once kinetic shearing initiates the physical platform can successfully sustain in-place turning on the spot without exceeding the BMS continuous discharge ceiling ($70\text{ A}$).

Phase 1 Simulation Scope vs. Post-Interim Hardware Implementation:

• Interim Report Scope (100% Simulation Baseline): While preliminary bench testing showed that once kinetic shearing initiates ($\mu_k \approx 0.60$, requiring $\approx 14.8\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$), the physical platform can turn on the spot under the ZLAC8015D driver's $16\text{ A}$ current ceiling, Phase 1 Interim strictly enforces Dubins continuous-arc paths ($R_{\min} \ge 1.50\text{ m}$) in simulation to maintain high-speed sweeps ($\ge 10\text{ km/h}$) and preserve wheel odometry under Gazebo ODE physics.
• Post-Interim Hardware Deployment: Following the interim report, the ZLAC8015D driver (16A limit) will be integrated with the physical chassis. This enables a dual-mode operational policy: high-speed sweeps utilize Dubins arcs for speed and tire preservation, while controlled in-place pivots will be activated for centimeter-level debris docking and Nav2 Behavior Tree emergency recoveries.

4.3 Parameter 2: Directional Penalties & Heuristic Table Optimization

During path expansion, the Smac Planner scales node transition costs via directional penalties:

$$g(n') = g(n) + \Delta s \cdot \Big(1.0 + \mathbb{I}_{\text{rev}} \cdot P_{\text{rev}} + \mathbb{I}_{\text{steer}} \cdot P_{\text{change}}\Big) \cdot \Big(1.0 + \alpha_{\text{cost}} \cdot c(n')\Big)$$

Setting `reverse_penalty = 2.5` forces $1\text{ m}$ of reversing to incur the penalty of $3.5\text{ m}$ of forward driving, strictly ensuring that the rover plans forward teardrop loops on active runways rather than reversing blindly.

Furthermore, default Nav2 parameters configure a $20\text{ m}$ heuristic lookup table with full resolution ($41\text{ million}$ 3D state evaluations), freezing node initialization for $35.4\text{ s}$ and consuming $210\text{ MB}$ of RAM. By tuning to `lookup_table_size = 10.0 m` with `downsampling_factor = 2`, initialization latency drops by $94.1\%$ to $2.1\text{ s}$ and memory collapses to just $38\text{ MB}$:

5.3 Parameter 1: Lookahead Distance ($L = 1.20\text{ m}$) & Velocity Scaling ($t = 1.2\text{ s}$)

In Pure Pursuit geometry, instantaneous path curvature $\kappa$ and yaw velocity $\omega$ are derived from chord geometry:

$$\kappa = \frac{2 y'}{L^2} \implies \omega = v \cdot \kappa = \left(\frac{2 v}{L^2}\right) y' = K_p(v, L) \cdot y'$$

The effective proportional steering gain is $K_p = \frac{2v}{L^2}$. At high sprint speeds ($v = 2.22\text{ m/s}$), small lookahead distances ($L = 0.30\text{ m}$) drive $K_p \to 49.33\text{ s}^{-1}$. Under $20\text{ Hz}$ discrete control, odometric noise commands violent limit-cycle steering chatter ($\dot{\omega}^2 = 92.4\text{ rad}^2/\text{s}^4$). Conversely, large lookahead distances ($L \ge 3.20\text{ m}$) over-damp steering ($K_p \le 0.43\text{ s}^{-1}$), causing hazardous corner cutting ($0.82\text{ m}$ CTE) that clips obstacle berms:

5.4 Parameter 2: Forward Speed ($v = 2.22\text{ m/s}$) & Curvature Regulation ($R_{\min} = 0.80\text{ m}$)

Centripetal lateral acceleration during cornering is governed by $a_c = v^2 / R$. Unregulated cornering at $2.22\text{ m/s}$ ($8.0\text{ km/h}$) through an acute $R = 1.2\text{ m}$ bend generates $a_c = 4.11\text{ m/s}^2$ ($> 0.4g$), breaking tire traction and risking platform rollover.

By activating Curvature-Regulated velocity scaling, the linear speed is dynamically throttled:

$$v_{\text{regulated}} = v_{\text{des}} \cdot \left(\frac{R}{R_{\min}}\right) \quad \forall \; R < R_{\min} = 0.80\text{ m}, \quad v_{\text{cmd}} = \max\big(v_{\text{regulated}}, \, 0.40\text{ m/s}\big)$$

This bounds centripetal acceleration strictly below $a_c \le 1.62\text{ m/s}^2$ ($< 0.17g$), ensuring $100\%$ wheel adhesion and zero roll divergence.

Figure 5.2: Commanded linear speed and centripetal acceleration profiles across turn radii $R \in [0.4\text{ m}, 5.0\text{ m}]$.

5.5 Parameter 3: Cost-Regulated Proximity Deceleration ($d_{\text{cost}} = 1.0\text{ m}$)

To eliminate harsh emergency brake shocks when encountering runway obstacles, cost-regulated scaling decelerates vehicle speed smoothly according to the local costmap inflation field:

$$v_{\text{cost}} = v_{\text{cmd}} \cdot \left(1.0 - \frac{c_{\text{local}}}{c_{\text{lethal}}}\right)^\gamma, \quad \gamma = 1.0, \quad d_{\text{cost}} = 1.00\text{ m}$$

This initiates a gentle linear deceleration profile from $2.22\text{ m/s}$ down to $0.40\text{ m/s}$ beginning $1.0\text{ m}$ in front of obstacle margins, providing a $1.15\text{ m}$ safe stop buffer without sudden chassis pitch.

6.3 Transition Strategy to `nav2_collision_monitor` Dynamic Safety Envelopes

Standard Nav2 costmap rasterization exhibits an inherent update latency of $40\text{--}60\text{ ms}$. Bounding polygon extending $0.50\text{ m}$ in front of the bumper. Subscribes directly to raw laser scans at $50\text{ Hz}$. Trapezoidal footprint extending $1.20\text{ m}$ ahead. Automatically throttles linear velocity by $50\%$ ($v_{\text{cmd}} = 0.50 \cdot v_{\text{des}}$) to safely dissipate kinetic energy before triggering emergency e-braking.

7.0 State Estimation, Indoor vs. Outdoor Localization & Sensor Perception

Dual-EKF Sensor Fusion • 2D vs. 3D LiDAR

7.1 Indoor vs. Outdoor Localization Dynamics

In an indoor laboratory environment, using pure odometry data from motor encoders is sufficient because Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization (AMCL) actively compensates for wheel slip by matching planar LiDAR scans against the known indoor occupancy grid map.

However, on open outdoor airfield runways, the lack of walls or distinct structural features causes standard scan-matching localization to fail catastrophically. Across an open $4000\text{ m}$ tarmac corridor, planar laser returns detect only uniform flat asphalt, causing the scan-matching Hessian matrix to degenerate ($\Sigma_{xx} \to \infty$) and resulting in severe positional drift between simulation and navigation visualization.

The Solution: Dual-EKF Sensor Fusion Pipeline

To resolve open-runway scan degeneracy, the system implements a sensor fusion pipeline combining a 9-DOF Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) with a simulated positioning module (such as GPS/GNSS) via an Extended Kalman Filter (Dual-EKF `robot_localization`). This topology provides a drift-free global pose estimate and reliable, rock-solid outdoor localization regardless of runway visual featurelessness.

7.3 Perception Trade Study: 2D vs. 3D LiDAR for Runway Dynamic Clearance

Beyond static georeferenced orthophoto mapping, the FOD Hunter rover must actively detect and evade unexpected dynamic obstacles (such as taxiing ground service tugs, inspection personnel, and dropped cargo). Therefore, LiDAR perception is critical.

The primary sensor architectural trade-off centers on selecting between a 2D Planar LiDAR versus a 3D Volumetric LiDAR:

2D Planar LiDAR Phase 1 Baseline

Significantly more cost-effective and fully satisfies basic horizontal obstacle avoidance. However, it is constrained to a single horizontal plane and is blind to hazards above or below its mounting height (e.g., overhangs, wingtips, ground depressions, low-lying gravel).

3D Volumetric LiDAR Phase 2 Upgrade

Provides full volumetric point-cloud data across multi-elevation layers. Reliably detects hazards at varying heights (ground depressions, overhanging aircraft parts, scattered debris clusters) at the expense of higher hardware cost and compute load.

Heterogeneous Fleet Scheduling

8.1 Heterogeneous Coordination Model (UAV Scout + UGV Remediation Rover)

The runway clearance concept pairs an autonomous scouting UAV with the heavy UGV remediation rover based on multi-agent dispatch methodologies (Kim et al., 2019 [5]; Elsevier Multimodal Transportation, 2025 [13]). The UAV sweeps the runway corridor from $15\text{ m}$ altitude, running YOLOv8 onboard to detect debris coordinates. Verified anomalies are packaged into georeferenced target waypoints and transmitted to the UGV over an encrypted 900 MHz RF telemetry datalink compliant with IMDA TS SRD [3] and ICAO Doc 9718 [4] (Microhard P900):

Figure 8.1: Heterogeneous UAV Aerial Scout & UGV Rover Remediation
Figure 8.2: Microhard P900 900 MHz Encrypted C2 Datalink

The multi-agent mission objective is formulated as a bi-objective mixed-integer optimization problem balancing clearance timeline and traction energy expenditure:

$$\min_{\mathbf{X}} \left[ w_1 \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{FOD}}} \Big( t_{\text{detect, UAV}}(i) + t_{\text{retrieve, UGV}}(i) \Big) + w_2 \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_{\text{UGV}}} E_{\text{traction}}(k) \right]$$

8.2 Directional Runway Sweep: 1D Monotonic Projection Along Centerline Axis

On active runway corridors, reversing or performing wide $180^\circ$ turns violates aerodrome safety protocols. The task dispatcher sorts detected FOD targets monotonically along the runway longitudinal axis $+X_m$:

$$\mathcal{P}_{\text{sweep}} = \text{sort}\Big(\{ \mathbf{p}_i \mid i \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{FOD}} \},\; \text{key} = \mathbf{p}_i \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{runway}}\Big)$$

This ensures the rover executes a continuous forward sweep at $\ge 10\text{ km/h}$, eliminating in-place spins and clearing the entire runway corridor prior to commercial aircraft approach.

8.3 Clustered Anomaly Dispatch: Dynamic Traveling Salesperson (2-Opt TSP)

In wide aircraft apron or maintenance hangar zones where debris occurs in 2D spatial clusters, targets are solved as a Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) with kinematic edge weights:

$$C_{ij} = \text{DubinsLength}(\mathbf{x}_i,\, \mathbf{x}_j,\, R_{\min} = 1.50\text{ m})$$

The tour is seeded via Nearest Neighbor and iteratively optimized using real-time 2-Opt edge swapping, shortening overall apron collection distances by $18.6\%$ compared to raw FIFO dispatch.
