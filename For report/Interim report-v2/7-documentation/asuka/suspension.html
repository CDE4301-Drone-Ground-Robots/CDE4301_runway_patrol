SECTION 4.1.1

Requirements and Constraints Involved in Design

Asuka

The suspension subsystem for the 65 kg autonomous runway FOD patrol UGV is governed by five core engineering constraints:

  1. Natural frequency upper ceiling: Frequency < 10 Hz. This project-advisor requirement ensures mechanical low-pass filtering and keeps suspension resonance well below optical sensor and LiDAR frame rates of 10-30 Hz.
  2. Stroke budget: Maximum 20 mm, set by the physical compression travel limit of the selected shock absorbers.
  3. Static operating sag: 40%-50% of stroke. Static deflection under gravity must sit near 8-10 mm to centre the shock piston and provide equal headroom for bump compression and droop travel.
  4. Operating speed: 10-15 km/h, or 2.8-4.2 m/s. Crossing asphalt expansion joints and runway seams at 4 m/s creates high-frequency impulses that must be dissipated without chassis bouncing.
  5. Corner mass budget: 16.25 kg per corner, calculated from the 65 kg UGV mass divided evenly across four wheels.
SECTION 4.1.2

Importance of the Suspension Subsystem

On an autonomous runway inspection robot, the suspension is a mission-enabling subsystem with four vital roles:

Perception deck and sensor protection
High-frequency frame chatter induces point-cloud jitter on 3D LiDARs and motion blur on optical-flow cameras. The suspension acts as a low-pass filter below 10 Hz.
Vacuum intake hood and suction seal
Vacuum retrieval depends on consistent ground clearance to preserve the high-speed air boundary layer. Chassis bounce breaks the seal and reduces debris-lifting capability.
Tire traction and skid-steer control
A four-wheel differential-drive robot turns by forcing tires to slide laterally. Suspension maintains normal force Fz > 0 over bumps, preventing wheel lift, motor overspeed, and odometry slip.
Structural fatigue and shock dissipation
The suspension absorbs transient kinetic energy and protects battery packs, motor gearboxes, frame joints, and other internal components from impact spikes.

First Principles Approach

Assuming rigid tire behaviour, the single-corner system reduces to a 1 Degree of Freedom mass-spring-damper system. The sprung mass is connected to the road profile through the spring ks and damper cs.

Applying Newton's Second Law:

$$m\ddot{x}_s(t)+c_s(\dot{x}_s(t)-\dot{r}(t))+k_s(x_s(t)-r(t))=0$$
$$(ms^2+c_ss+k_s)X(s)=(c_ss+k_s)R(s)$$

The transfer function is:

$$G(s)=\frac{X(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{c_ss+k_s}{ms^2+c_ss+k_s}=\frac{2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}{s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2}$$

Using the canonical parameters:

$$\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k_s}{m}},\qquad 2\zeta\omega_n=\frac{c_s}{m},\qquad f_d=f_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$$

The steady-state displacement is calculated from $x_{steady}=F_{corner}/k_{spring}$, with the corner force identified in the design notes as approximately 154.42 N.

SECTION 4.2.2

Motion Ratio and Wheel Rate

When the shock is installed at an angle $\theta$, the motion ratio relates spring displacement to wheel displacement. The wheel rate is reduced by the square of the motion ratio:

$$MR=\frac{\Delta z_{spring}}{\Delta z_{wheel}},\qquad k_w=k_s\cos^2\theta$$

The project calculations use a motion ratio of 0.95, giving:

$$k_w=k_s\times MR^2$$

SECTION 4.3

Suspension Design Figures

The PDF identifies the double wishbone as the selected suspension architecture. The trailing-arm figure is retained as a backup concept, while the MacPherson strut is rejected because of shock loading under skid steering.
