SECTION 1.0

Requirements from Drive Speed

Kinematics & Compute Budget

Every camera and compute limit starts from how fast the robot moves.

$$v = \frac{\text{RPM} \times 2\pi r}{60} = \frac{400 \times 2\pi(0.1)}{60} = 4.19\text{ m/s} \approx 15.1\text{ km/h}$$
In plain words: wheel rotation speed times tire circumference gives the robot's ground velocity.

SECTION 2.0

Camera Geometry & Ground Sampling Distance

Optics & Projection

2.1 Focal Length in Pixels ($f_x, f_y$)

The pinhole camera model converts physical field-of-view angles into discrete sensor pixel dimensions. For a sensor of width $W_{\text{sensor}}$ and height $H_{\text{sensor}}$, the pixel focal lengths scale inversely with the tangent of half the angular field of view. Because pixels on our sensors are square, $f_x$ and $f_y$ share the same value.

$$f_x = \frac{W_{\text{sensor}}}{2 \cdot \tan\left(\frac{\text{HFOV}}{2}\right)}, \quad f_y = \frac{H_{\text{sensor}}}{2 \cdot \tan\left(\frac{\text{VFOV}}{2}\right)}$$
In plain words: how many pixels represent the lens's optical focal length; higher means more zoom.

2.2 Oblique Slant Range & Ground Incidence

Because the camera sits $0.45\text{ m}$ off the ground and points $20^\circ$ downward, it views the tarmac at an angle. The true line-of-sight distance $R$ to any runway point $d$ ahead of the lens follows the Pythagorean theorem. The incidence angle $\psi$ tells us how steeply that line of sight strikes the flat runway.

$$R = \sqrt{H^2 + d^2}, \qquad \psi = \arctan\left(\frac{H}{d}\right)$$
In plain words: true line-of-sight distance to the ground and how steeply the camera looks down at it.

2.3 Ground Sample Distance (GSD)

Ground Sample Distance measures how many millimetres of runway one pixel covers. Looking forward at a shallow angle stretches the vertical footprint along the driving direction by $1 / \sin\psi$. Across the direction of travel, resolution stays sharper because horizontal ray geometry is not foreshortened.

$$\text{GSD}_{\text{across}} = \frac{R \cdot 1000}{f_x}\ (\text{mm/px}), \qquad \text{GSD}_{\text{along}} = \frac{\text{GSD}_{\text{across}}}{\sin(\psi)}\ (\text{mm/px})$$
In plain words: how many millimetres of runway one pixel covers; smaller is sharper.

SECTION 3.0

Pixels the Model Actually Sees

Input Resolution Scaling

The model shrinks each frame before detecting. This table shows a 30 mm item at the image centre (0.45 m, 20°).

Camera Native ($W \times L\text{ px}$) at 640 input at 1088 input
IMX568 60 × 21 16 × 5 27 × 9
UGREEN 36 × 12 12 × 4 21 × 7
e-CAM56 22 × 7 6 × 2 10 × 3

YOLO's finest layer reads the image in 8 × 8 px blocks. At 640 the item is only a couple of blocks wide, which is why the model trained at 1088 found more fasteners (see YOLO model comparison page: 87.7% → 97.0% mAP@50).

Why not the wide e-CAM56?

Its 104.8° lens spreads pixels over a wide area, so a 30 mm item gets only 22 px across even at full resolution.

SECTION 4.0

Motion Blur, Shutter & Lighting

Exposure Dynamics
$$\Delta x = v \cdot t_{\text{exp}} \le \text{GSD}_{\text{along}} \implies t_{\text{exp}} \le \frac{\text{GSD}_{\text{along}}}{v}$$

Worked (IMX568): centre 1.45 mm/px ÷ 4.19 m/s ≈ 346 µs; near edge 0.39 mm/px ÷ 4.19 m/s ≈ 93 µs.

In plain words: The near edge is closest to the camera, so motion there smears the most pixels; it sets the limit.
GLOBAL SHUTTER

IMX568 Global Shutter

A rolling shutter reads the frame line by line over several milliseconds; at 4.19 m/s the robot moves centimetres during one frame, skewing the image. The IMX568 captures every pixel at once.

LIGHTING (PLANNED)

Synchronised Strobe

Exposures under 100 µs need strong, controlled light; a synchronised LED strobe is planned and will be sized in Semester 2.

Ideas to evaluate: CPL filters & DOL-HDR (unverified)

Wet runway puddles can produce specular glare, and bright tropical sun creates harsh shadows in asphalt grooves. Circular Polarizing (CPL) filters and Digital Overlap HDR (DOL-HDR) are under evaluation as unverified options to be tested if glare causes false alarms in field trials.
