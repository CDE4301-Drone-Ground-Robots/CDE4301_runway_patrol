2. Suspension System: Architecture Trade-Off & Comparison
Terrain Compliance

High-speed runway operation ($\ge 10\text{ km/h}$) introduces high-frequency vibration from asphalt aggregate texture and longitudinal shock from concrete expansion grooves. Four suspension architectures were comparatively evaluated:

Suspension Type Kinematic Architecture Key Strengths Limitations / Drawbacks Evaluation Status
MacPherson Strut Single lower control arm with integrated tall telescoping spring-damper Compact lateral width, low component count Tall vertical height conflicts with low chassis CG; lateral side-loads cause strut friction REJECTED
Double Wishbone Upper and lower parallel A-arms with coilover shock Exceptional camber angle control, optimal tire road contact patch High mechanical complexity (multiple ball joints and pivots), wide lateral space consumption encroaches on vacuum duct EVALUATED
Linear Suspension (Guide Pillar) Pure vertical slide shafts with compression springs Simple vertical motion, zero camber angle deviation Severe mechanical binding (jamming) under horizontal braking/cornering loads; sand/grit abrasion REJECTED
Trailing Arm Suspension Longitudinal swing arm pivoting perpendicular to travel with angled coilover damper Superior longitudinal shock absorption over expansion cracks; compact vertical profile; rugged pivot bearings; zero binding Minor camber change during full compression (negligible on flat runways) SELECTED
Selection Justification: The Trailing Arm Suspension was selected because airport runways are predominantly flat with sharp transverse expansion cracks and grooving. Trailing arms naturally rotate backward and upward upon striking cracks, dampening shocks without the binding risk of linear slides or the packaging bulk of double wishbones.
3. Motor & Powertrain Requirements: Mathematical Sizing
Powertrain Engineering

To achieve the operational target of $\ge 10\text{ km/h}$ while climbing runway access ramps and handling full payload mass, powertrain parameters were calculated:

1. Kinematic Velocity & RPM Calculation

Target Velocity: $v = 10\text{ km/h} \approx 2.78\text{ m/s}$
Drive Wheel Radius: $r = 0.10\text{ m}$ ($200\text{ mm}$ all-terrain tire)
Angular Velocity: $\omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{2.78}{0.10} = 27.8\text{ rad/s}$
Required Wheel Output Speed = $\approx 265\text{ RPM}$

2. Tractive Force & Torque Budget

Vehicle Gross Mass: $M = 40\text{ kg}$
Ramp Incline Angle: $\theta = 15^\circ$ ($F_{\text{grade}} = M g \sin 15^\circ \approx 101.5\text{ N}$)
Rolling Resistance + Acceleration: $F_{\text{rolling}} + F_{\text{acc}} \approx 28.5\text{ N}$
Total Tractive Force: $F_{\text{total}} \approx 130\text{ N}$
Total Torque $\tau = F \times r = 130 \times 0.10 = 13.0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$

3. Selected Motor & Driver Hardware

Hub Motors: Dual ZLLG80ASM25 0-HT V2.0 8-inch high-torque direct-drive hub motors (zero gearbox backlash, >88% efficiency).
Servo Driver: ZLAC8015D V4.2 dual-channel DC brushless servo driver via CAN bus.
Bus Voltage: 48V LiFePO4 bus (up to 800W peak mechanical output).
Suspension & Mounts: Coilover suspension shock absorbers, PETG CF structural brackets, and TPU 98A dampeners.

4. Steering System Architecture Comparison
Kinematic Analysis

Airside FOD extraction demands swift transit down runway centerlines combined with agile localized maneuvering above small flagged coordinates. Three candidate steering architectures were benchmarked:

Steering Architecture Kinematic Model Turning Radius Tarmac Traction & Durability Mechanical Complexity Selection Decision
Differential Drive Dual independent drive wheels with passive casters / trailing support Zero Radius ($R = 0\text{ m}$, in-place spin) Excellent (Pneumatic rubber tires on asphalt) Minimal (No steering servos or linkages) SELECTED
Ackermann Steering Steering knuckles & tie rods (Car-like geometry) Large ($R_{\text{min}} > 1.5\text{ m}$) High high-speed lateral stability High (Tie rods, servos, ball joints prone to play) REJECTED
Mecanum Drive 4 independent $45^\circ$ angled roller wheels Zero Radius (Omnidirectional holonomic) Very Poor (Tarmac grit jams rollers; high vibration) Moderate (4 independent motors required) REJECTED

Why Differential Drive is Optimal for Runway Patrol:

  • In-Place Zero Pivot: Allows the UGV to arrive at a target coordinate, rotate precisely in place, align the front vacuum hood directly over the FOD, and reverse without requiring multi-point turns.
  • Outdoor Durability: Uses rugged all-terrain pneumatic rubber tires with high friction coefficients ($\mu \approx 0.85$ on asphalt), unlike fragile Mecanum rollers that fail on outdoor grit.
  • Nav2 Native Compatibility: Standard differential drive inverse kinematics are directly integrated into the ROS 2 Nav2 controller plugins (DWBLocalPlanner and SmacPlanner2D).
